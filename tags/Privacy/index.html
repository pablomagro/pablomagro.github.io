Data Removal and Digital Footprint Tools: DeleteMe vs Incogni vs Redact

Three services that tackle the same underlying problem - too much of your personal data is public - from different angles: data broker removal and social media cleanup.

The Tools at a Glance

Tool Focus Pricing Link
DeleteMe Data broker & search engine removal Subscription, price on request joindeleteme.com
Incogni Automated data broker removal From ~$7.19/mo (annual) incogni.com
Redact Social media history cleanup Free tier; Premium $7.99/mo redact.dev

Tool Breakdown

DeleteMe - Data Broker & Search Engine Removal

joindeleteme.com

DeleteMe is one of the longest-running personal data removal services, having completed over 100 million opt-out removals since 2010. You submit your personal details and a human-assisted team locates and removes them from 50+ data broker sites - names, addresses, phone numbers, relatives, social media links, and more. It runs as an ongoing subscription with continuous scans and removal reports delivered within 7 days of signup, and it also offers business plans aimed at reducing executive doxxing and social engineering risk.

Incogni - Automated Data Broker Removal

incogni.com

Incogni takes a similar approach to DeleteMe but leans fully automated, covering 420+ data brokers directly and up to 3,420+ sites through its Custom Removals add-on. It sends removal requests on your behalf and re-scans regularly to catch data that gets re-collected over time. Plans scale from a single-person Standard tier to Family options covering up to 5 household members, with a dashboard and periodic privacy reports to track progress. It’s generally the cheaper of the two broker-removal services and includes a 30-day money-back guarantee.

Redact - Social Media History Cleanup

redact.dev

Redact addresses a different problem: your own social media history. It connects to 35+ platforms and lets you bulk-delete old posts, comments, likes, and direct messages by keyword, date range, or content type, all processed locally on your device rather than uploaded to Redact’s servers. A “Disappearing Mode” auto-deletes new posts after they age past a set threshold, and a local archive keeps a private copy before anything is removed. The free tier covers recent Twitter/X, Discord, and Facebook content; Premium ($7.99/mo) unlocks unlimited history and more platforms.

How They Differ

  • DeleteMe and Incogni solve the same problem - your data sitting on people-search and data broker sites - but DeleteMe uses more human involvement while Incogni is built for full automation at a lower price point.
  • Redact solves an entirely different problem: content you posted yourself, across social platforms, rather than data aggregated by third parties.

Used together, DeleteMe/Incogni handle the broker side and Redact handles the platforms you actually post to - there’s little overlap between them.

Strengths

  • Ongoing, recurring removal rather than a one-time cleanup, since brokers re-collect data over time
  • Redact’s local-processing model avoids handing your social media credentials’ data to another server
  • Family/household plans (Incogni) and business plans (DeleteMe) extend coverage beyond a single person

Trade-offs

  • None of these guarantee 100% removal - data brokers can and do re-list information
  • Recurring subscription cost for as long as you want protection to continue
  • Redact’s free tier is narrow (30-day window, 3 platforms); real usefulness requires Premium

How to Disable the Hidden Background Screen-Analysis Feature on Android

Recent Android updates have quietly added a background permission that can analyze and record what’s happening on your screen - here’s how to find it and turn it off.

Originally spotted in a Spanish-language Android tutorial video circulating on social media.

What’s Going On

Newer versions of Android ship with a background feature/permission that has access to analyze - and in some cases record - screen activity in real time. It’s typically framed as a security feature (part of Google Play Protect’s live threat and scam detection, which watches the screen for signs of fraud during calls or screen-sharing sessions), but it runs silently in the background and isn’t always obvious to the average user.

The video (in Spanish, titled “¡Mucho cuidado con lo último que ha llegado a tu Android!”) walks through:

  1. Detecting the setting - navigating Android’s settings menu to find the hidden screen-recording/screen-analysis permission.
  2. Disabling the feature - revoking the permission and turning off the background service to restore full privacy over what happens on your screen.

Why It Matters

Even when the stated intent is protective (catching scams in progress, flagging fraudulent apps), a permission that can see everything on your screen is a meaningful privacy trade-off - and most users never opted in knowingly. If you’d rather make that call yourself, it’s worth checking your settings.

Where to Look

On most recent Android versions, the relevant toggle lives under:

  • Settings → Google → Google Play Protect / Play Protect settings - look for “scan apps with Play Protect” and any live/real-time protection options.
  • Settings → Security & privacy → More security settings - some OEMs (Samsung, Xiaomi) surface it here instead.
  • Settings → Apps → Special app access → Screen recording / Device admin apps - check what has been granted screen-capture access.

Exact menu names vary by Android version and manufacturer, so the safest approach is to search your settings app for “screen” or “scan” and review anything with access to on-screen content.

Takeaway

This isn’t a malware warning - it’s a legitimate (if under-communicated) Android security feature. But “legitimate” doesn’t mean “you have to keep it on.” If background screen analysis isn’t something you want running, a few taps in Settings will disable it.
